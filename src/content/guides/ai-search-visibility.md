Search results increasingly arrive as an assembled answer rather than a list of links to click through. That is a real shift for anyone who has been optimising for a ranking position, and it deserves a calm, specific account rather than either dismissal or hype.

## What actually changed

Traditional search returns a ranked list; the searcher picks a result and clicks through. AI-generated answers — Google's AI Overviews, and chat assistants like ChatGPT or Gemini answering a search-shaped question directly — assemble a response from multiple sources and present it before or instead of that list.

The consequence for a business is direct: a page can be one of the sources an answer draws from and still receive no click, because the searcher's question was already answered on the results page. Ranking well and being visited are no longer the same outcome.

## What appears to drive citation

Some of what follows is established by observation of how these systems behave; some is reasonable inference from how they are built. Both are labelled.

**Established:** pages that state claims clearly, in extractable form — a direct answer near the top, clean headings, tables for comparative data — get cited more often than pages that bury the same information in dense paragraphs.

**Established:** corroboration across multiple independent sources appears to increase the likelihood that a claim gets cited, consistent with these systems favouring claims that several sources agree on.

**Inference, not established fact:** the exact weighting a specific system gives to any one signal — page authority, freshness, structured data — is not published, and claims that state a precise formula should be treated as guesses, including this guide's own account of what appears to matter.

## Why structure matters more, not less

If a page has to be understood by both a human reader and a system extracting claims from it automatically, clear structure stops being a nice-to-have. Direct answers, plain headings that state what the section covers, and short paragraphs are already good writing practice; AI-assembled answers raise the cost of skipping them, because unclear structure now means the page competes worse in two channels instead of one.

## How long before I see anything?

This area is in progress, not finished. AI Overviews continue to change in coverage and format, and treating any current snapshot as permanent is a mistake.

What to do now: fix structure and clarity, because that work pays off regardless of how AI search evolves further, and it is the same work that improves conventional ranking. What to wait on: platform-specific tactics aimed at gaming a particular system's current behaviour, because that behaviour is the part most likely to change without notice.

<!-- frame:risk -->
## Could this damage my site or waste my money?

The real risk here is over-correction: abandoning working, foundational practice to chase a target that is still moving. A business that drops clear writing and solid technical SEO in favour of unproven "AI optimisation" tactics is trading a known return for an unknown one.

The safer position is treating AI visibility as an extension of existing good practice — structured data, clear claims, corroborated content — rather than a separate discipline requiring separate spend.
