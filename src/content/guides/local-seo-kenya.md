Local search is the layer most Kenyan businesses meet first, because it is where a phone-based, location-anchored search culture puts the most weight. Getting found in Maps and the local pack runs on a different set of signals than ordinary web ranking, and most of them are within a business's own control.

## What decides local pack ranking

Google names three factors for local ranking: relevance, distance, and prominence.

**Relevance** is how well a business profile matches what was searched. Category selection and business description drive this most.

**Distance** is how far the business is from the searcher or the searched location. A business cannot move its physical premises closer to every searcher, and does not need to — distance is one factor among three, not a veto.

**Prominence** is how well-known and well-regarded the business is, drawn from reviews, links, articles, and directory listings. This is the factor with the most room to improve deliberately.

What a business can move: category accuracy, profile completeness, review volume and response rate, citation consistency. What it cannot move: its physical location relative to any given searcher, or the raw fact of being newer than an established competitor.

## Reviews: volume, recency, and response rate

Review count matters, but review velocity — how steadily new reviews arrive — matters more than the raw total. A business with 40 reviews and three new ones this month reads as active; a business with 200 reviews and none in a year reads as dormant, even though the total is higher.

Responding to reviews, including negative ones, is a visible signal of an active, attentive business, and Kenyan directories weight recency more heavily than most owners assume.

## NAP consistency across directories

NAP — name, address, phone number — needs to match exactly across every place a business is listed: Google Business Profile, Facebook, directory sites, the business's own website footer. This is unusually messy in Kenya, where a business might be listed under three slightly different names or phone numbers across old directory signups, abandoned listings, and its current details. Inconsistent NAP data confuses the signal that ties all those mentions together into one prominence score.

## Is this for a business my size?

Local SEO suits any business with a physical location or a defined service area — a shop, a clinic, a workshop, a technician who travels to the customer. It does not suit a business with no local component: an ecommerce operation shipping nationwide with no storefront gets little from Maps optimisation, because there is no "near me" search to win.

Size is not the deciding factor. A single-location business with an accurate, active profile regularly outperforms a larger competitor with a neglected one.

## Does this actually work?

Google Business Profile's own insights are the measurement surface for local SEO, and they answer the question directly: how many people saw the profile, and through what kind of search.

Insights split views into **discovery** searches — someone found the business by searching for the category or service, not the business name — and **direct** searches, where someone already knew the name. A rising share of discovery searches is the clearest sign that local optimisation is working, because it means new people are finding the business who did not already know it existed.
