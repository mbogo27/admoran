A real audit is a prioritised diagnosis of a specific site. It is not a tool's PDF export with a score out of 100 — that distinction is the entire difference between an audit worth paying for and one that is not.

## What actually gets checked

**Crawlability.** Whether search engines can reach every page that matters, and whether `robots.txt` or meta directives are blocking anything unintentionally.

**Indexation.** Whether pages that should be indexed are indexed, and whether pages that should not be — thin, duplicate, or staging content — are leaking into the index.

**Site architecture.** How pages link to each other, whether important pages are reachable within a few clicks, and whether the structure reflects how the business actually organises its offering.

**Speed on mobile connections.** Load time and responsiveness tested against realistic Kenyan mobile conditions, not just a fast office connection.

**Structured data.** Whether schema markup is present, correctly formatted, and matched to what the page actually contains.

**Content overlap.** Pages competing with each other for the same search intent, which look like strength but function as internal competition.

## Diagnosis versus automated score

An automated score report weights what is measurable, not what matters. A tool can count missing alt text or flag a page as "too short" in seconds; it cannot judge whether that missing alt text is on a decorative image nobody searches for, or whether that short page is short because it is a clean, complete answer to a narrow question. The score treats both cases the same, because it can only count what is easy to count.

A real diagnosis starts from the score's raw data but adds judgement: which of the flagged issues would actually move something if fixed, given this site's specific traffic, competition, and goals, and which are cosmetic.

## What a good audit deliverable contains

A usable audit deliverable has four parts: the findings themselves, a severity rating for each, an estimate of the effort to fix it, and a prioritised order to work through them in. An audit that lists forty issues with no severity or order is not more useful than one that lists five — it is often less useful, because it gives no guidance on where to start.

## Does this actually work?

A genuine audit should be independently verifiable — every finding in it should be something the business can go and check itself, directly. A claim that "the sitemap is missing" can be checked by looking for it. A claim that "page speed is poor on mobile" can be checked with a free public tool. If a finding cannot be checked without trusting the auditor's word alone, that finding has not actually been demonstrated.

## Could this damage my site or waste my money?

Two risks sit specifically at the audit stage. The first is acting on findings out of order — fixing a low-severity cosmetic issue before a high-severity indexing block, because the list had no priority attached. The second is a fix that breaks something else: a redirect change that creates a loop, a canonical tag applied incorrectly, a robots directive edited without checking what else it affects. Both risks are reduced by the same thing — a properly prioritised, specific deliverable rather than a generic checklist applied without judgement.
