Kenya's SEO market has a real credibility problem, and pretending otherwise helps nobody looking to hire honestly. The following are specific, checkable signals, not a generic warning to "be careful."

## Guaranteed rankings

No one can honestly guarantee a specific ranking position, because ranking depends on factors outside any provider's control — competitor behaviour, algorithm changes, the searcher's own history and location. A guarantee is either a misunderstanding of how search works or a deliberate promise the provider knows they cannot reliably keep. Either way, it is a reason to ask more questions before signing.

## Vanity metrics in reporting

A report showing rankings for keywords nobody searches, or traffic numbers with no source breakdown, is easy to make look impressive and easy to produce without doing anything that matters. Ask specifically for Search Console impressions and clicks on terms with real search volume, and for a traffic breakdown by source — organic search should be identifiable and growing, not folded into an undifferentiated total.

## Bought links and private blog networks

Paid or bulk-acquired backlinks, and links from private blog networks built specifically to sell links, violate search engine guidelines and carry real risk of a manual or algorithmic penalty. When a network like this gets identified and devalued — which happens periodically — every site that relied on it loses the benefit at once, sometimes with an additional penalty attached.

## No account access, or unverifiable reports

A provider who will not give the business direct access to its own Search Console and Analytics accounts, or who provides reports that cannot be checked independently, is asking to be trusted rather than verified. There is no legitimate reason to withhold access to accounts that belong to the client.

## Contracts with no defined deliverables

Covered in more detail in [hiring an SEO](/guides/hiring-an-seo): a contract for "monthly SEO" with no named deliverables gives no way to measure whether the engagement is working, and no basis to raise a problem before months have passed.

## Does this actually work?

The specific questions to ask, and what a good answer sounds like: "Can I have direct access to Search Console?" — yes, immediately, with no hesitation. "What exactly will you deliver this month?" — a specific, named list, not "ongoing optimisation." "How do you build links?" — a description of outreach, content, or relationships, not a number of links per month at a fixed price. Hesitation, vagueness, or a pivot to talking about rankings instead of answering the access question directly are all answers in themselves.

## Could this damage my site or waste my money?

Recovery from link-based damage is slow, expensive, and sometimes incomplete. It typically involves identifying every low-quality or purchased link, requesting removal directly from site owners — a process with a low response rate — and, where removal fails, filing a disavow request with Google. None of this restores lost time, and there is no guarantee the site fully recovers its prior position even once the underlying links are addressed. Avoiding the practice in the first place is considerably cheaper than reversing it.
